How a hash becomes a landscape

Every landscape here is a function of 256 bits. Text goes in, SHA-256 turns it into a hash, and the hash is read, field by field, as the shape of a ridge, the fork of a branch, the angle of the sun. Nothing is random and nothing is trained: the same words always draw the same picture, byte for byte.

Algorithms by Hyunwook Shin[1]

landscape=render(SHA-256(text))

The nine works below show the core of the method, one formula at a time. Each figure is drawn by this page, from the formulas on its placard, with fixed values in place of hash bits. The edge cases live in the full specification.

Room I

The ridge

Constrained midpoint displacement · Mountains, bits 48–135

A ridge starts as three points. Then every segment AB is split at its midpoint M, and the midpoint is pushed sideways, perpendicular to AB, by an amount the hash chooses. Each level halves the segments and adds finer detail: the coarse bits set the mountain, the fine bits its texture.

The push is bounded to a tenth of the segment, and narrowed on steep segments so the new point stays between its parents. The ridge can never fold back over itself. The bits are mapped into the allowed range rather than clamped after, so two hashes never collapse to one ridge.

P(t)=M+rt(−Δh,Δx)
The push, perpendicular to AB. With r = 0.1, |t| ≤ 1 keeps P within 10% of the segment's length, with no square root.
tmax=min(1,γΔx2r|Δh|)
On steep segments the range narrows, so P stays strictly between A and B from left to right (γ = 0.5).
t=tmax(2v2b−1−1)
The b bits for this point, read as a number v, spread evenly across the allowed range.

Room II

The split

The incoming slope · no bits; it follows the silhouette

A flat fill reads as a paper cutout. Real peaks have faces: the slope that catches the sun and the slope that turns away. So each sharp peak sends a line down into the mountain, toward the viewer, and that line splits the ridge into a lit half and a shaded half.

Only real peaks qualify: a rise into a fall that turns by more than 25°, tested without a square root. The cut always lies between the two slopes that meet at the peak; the peak's own height decides where.

(u·v)2<cos2α|u|2|v|2
With u = b − a and v = c − b, the path turns at b by more than α (or u · v < 0). For peaks, α = 25°.
d=d^L+f(d^R−d^L)
The cut's direction: a blend of the two slopes, a − b and c − b, each scaled so its larger component is 1.
f=0.1+0.8bhmod3737
Where between them, from the peak's height bh: kept away from both slopes, so a face never collapses to a sliver.

Room III

The facets

Triangulating the rock · no bits; the digits of π

Bare rock is faceted. Columns run down from the silhouette, leaning with the nearest peak's cut, and rows follow the silhouette down to the base, so the mesh lines up with the faces of Room II. Each cell splits into two triangles.

The points are nudged a little and given depth by the digits of π, so the mesh is irregular yet the same every time. Then each triangle is lit: a step lighter if it faces the sun, a step darker if it turns away, and left alone in between.

w(x)=11+(x−px300)2
How strongly a peak at px steers the column at x; a straight-down pull of weight 0.3 is added to the sum.
s=sign(n·L)(n·L)2|n|2|L|2
The signed squared cosine between a triangle's normal n and the light: Lambert's law, with no square root. Above 0.4 a facet lightens; below 0.05 it darkens.
L=(−dxℓ,1,0)
The light comes from the sun's side and from above, using the sun of Room VI.
subspec/mountains.md
Room IV

The tree

Recursive forks · Trees, bits 176–223

Every tree grows from a trunk by forks. A fork splits a branch into a large child, which carries on, and a small one, which turns away. The split ratio r sets their shares, and from it their angles and lengths: the more even the split, the wider the fork.

The first forks come from the hash. When its bits run out, the tree keeps growing by rule, stepping each new fork by the golden ratio so no two forks repeat in lockstep. The rotations themselves use a rational form of the angle, so no sine or cosine is ever computed.

(c,s)=(1−t21+t2,2t1+t2)
A rotation from t, the tangent of half the angle: exact, and with no trigonometry.
tlarge=tan22.5°·2(1−r)
The large child tilts by about 90°(1 − r), the small one by about 90°·r, to opposite sides. At r = 0.5 both are 45°.
uk={u+0.618…·(k+1)}
Fork k past the hash's own reuses an earlier fork's value u, shifted by the golden ratio; {·} keeps the fractional part.

Room V

The placement

Composition and scatter · Trees, bits 176–223; Vegetation, bits 224–247

Trees are not sprinkled at random. Their count and arrangement fix a composition, as a painter would: a leader at the center of a grove, members overlapping around it, one tree standing apart, and a straggler once the hill holds five or more. Open hillside is left on purpose.

Grass, petals, rosettes, or fallen leaves then fill the ground the trees leave. Each plant picks a gap between two trees, settles near its middle, and lands at a depth drawn so that more of them crowd the far rim, the way receding ground holds more in less space. Near ones grow larger, in the trees' perspective.

c=W(0.2+0.6{0.37+0.29a+0.41h})
The grove's center, from the arrangement bits a and the hill h. The isolated tree stands 0.38W away, on the side with more room.
x=eg+(eg+1−eg)1+u1−u22
Across a gap between trees: the difference of two uniform draws peaks in the middle and thins to nothing at each trunk.
h=0.97R(x)(1−u34)
Down the slope, from just below the rim R to the canvas bottom. The fourth power packs plants toward the rim and keeps the front open.
subspec/trees.md, subspec/vegetation.md
Room VI

Sun and moon

An angle without trigonometry · Celestial, bits 40–47; Global, bits 0–1

Neither is ever drawn. Eight bits place the light, four for its direction around the ground and four for its height, and the scene shows it only through what it lights: faces, warm skies, and long shadows falling toward the viewer. In one scene in four it is night, and the moon takes the sun's place on the same angles: the same shadows and faces, in moonlit colors. By day, the two daylight bits nudge the sun on by thirds of a step.

Its angles are turned into vectors without sine or cosine. A line through (−1, 0) with slope q meets the unit circle at exactly the point at angle α, so a table of q values, given to four decimals, yields shadow directions of exactly unit length. The same answer comes out on every machine.

α=15°+10°a+10°3d,φ=20°+3°e
Azimuth from the left, turning behind the scene, and elevation above the ground. The daylight value d is 0 to 2 by day; at night (d = 3) the moon keeps the plain steps.
(dx,dy)=(1−q21+q2,2q1+q2),q=tanα2
The shadow's direction on the ground, away from the sun: the circle's rational parametrization.
ℓ=cotφ
Shadow length per unit of height, also from a table: 2.75 for the lowest sun, 0.47 for the highest.

Room VII

The shadow

Casting onto a curved hill · no bits of its own; it reads the sun

The front hill is a surface, not a flat backdrop. At depth f = 0 it follows the crest; toward the bottom of the canvas it flattens out. Each tree stands somewhere on it, and its trunk and branches are cast onto it, point by point, along the sun's direction.

Every tree shares one direction and one length per unit of height, so on the ground all shadows are parallel. On the canvas they are not: a tree near the rim casts a steep shadow down the slope, a tree near the bottom a flat, sideways one.

H(x,f)=(1−f)((1−f)S(x)+fSmin)
The ground's height at depth f, from the silhouette S: it hugs the crest at the top and settles toward the bottom.
x′=x+zℓdx,f′=fb+zℓdyG
Where a point a height z above the tree's base lands. Its height on the canvas is H(x′, f′).
H(xb,fb)=hb
A tree's base depth, solved by bisection: 50 halvings of the interval [0, 1].

Room VIII

The cloud

Five points, three refinements · Sky, bits 16–31

Sixteen bits set a trapezoid: where it sits, how wide and tall, how skewed. The top edge is always the longer, so clouds are flat-bottomed and wide. Five points are placed around its edge, one in each fifth of the way around.

Then the outline is refined three times. Between every pair of neighbors, four new points are dropped inside a narrow rhombus, so the edge gains billows at three scales without ever leaving the trapezoid. 5 points become 625, joined by smooth curves and softened by a blur.

si=i+0.2+0.6ui5P
Point i's distance around the perimeter P, starting from the top left; ui is a fixed scramble of the bits.
w(t)=0.15(1−|2t−1|)(2u′−1)
How far a new point, a fraction t of the way from A to B, moves sideways: at most 15% of |AB| in the middle, nothing at the ends. That is the rhombus.
g(x)∝e−x2/2σ2,σ=0.012W
A Gaussian blur, scaled to the cloud's width W, melts the edge into the sky.

Room IX

The palette

Color in a perceptual space · Mountains, Sky, Hills, Trees, Vegetation, Fog

Colors are chosen in OKLCH, a color space built so that equal steps look like equal steps: lightness L, chroma C, hue h. Each biome owns a family of hues: slate blues for Alpine, teal fading to blue for Temperate, lavender for Tundra, ochre and sand for Desert. A ridge's bits pick one shade within it.

Depth is kept by lightness alone. Each ridge has its own band, darkest in front and palest at the back, and the bands never overlap, so whatever the bits choose, the nearer mountain is darker, as haze makes it in the real world. And every one of the 256 shades must still be a distinct color once rounded to #RRGGBB, which the build checks.

L=L0+(L1−L0)i7,i∈{0,…,7}
One of 8 even steps within the ridge's lightness band; hue takes 8 steps and chroma 4, for 8 × 8 × 4 = 256 shades.
Lfront<Lmiddle<Lfar
Disjoint bands, [0.30, 0.48], [0.51, 0.65] and [0.68, 0.77]: depth order survives every choice of bits.
L=0.70+0.035l,C=0.03+0.007c,h=212°+6°k
The sky: 8 bits make 256 light blues, from 2 bits of lightness l, 3 of chroma c and 3 of hue k. A low sun then warms them.
subspec/mountains.md, subspec/sky.md

[1] Authorship: the design prompts behind these rooms

The algorithms in these rooms were designed by Hyunwook Shin, in conversation with Claude, an AI model by Anthropic, between October 18 and 21, 2026. The author set each rule: what the bits must preserve, how a ridge subdivides, how a tree forks, where the light comes from, how a cloud is built, and then judged every render and sent it back until it looked right. Claude worked out the remaining details, wrote the code and the wording of the specifications, and wrote this page. The full history is public in the commit log.

The author's prompts below are quoted verbatim, typos included, so the attribution can be checked. Long messages are excerpted; … marks a cut. They are a selection: the full exchange ran to about 250 messages.
